The Riemann Hypothesis, drawn with circles

The most famous problem in mathematics, explained with a compass, a ruler and one idea about rotation. No formulas needed until the very end.

By Sanjin Redzic · Interactive: every figure responds to the controls

STEP 1 · THE CIRCLES

One circle for every prime

Prime numbers are the whole numbers greater than 1 that can only be divided evenly by 1 and by themselves: 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, … Start at 3. Draw a circle of radius 3 standing on a line. Next to it, a circle of radius 5, placed so the two just touch. Then 7, then 11, and so on: one circle for every prime, each touching the one before.

Notice what sets each position: every circle is pushed along by all the circles before it. The exact step between neighbouring circles of radius p and q is 2√(pq), which is very close to p + q because neighbouring primes are close compared with their size. So a centre's distance along the line behaves like twice the running total of all the primes so far, not like the width of one circle. That running total is what we will follow.

Mark the centre of every circle. The primes jump around unpredictably, yet the centres line up surprisingly well along a smooth curve (dashed). That curve, x = 2·li(y²) − y − 3, comes from the prime number theorem from 1896 applied to the running total of the primes (li is the logarithmic integral, a standard smooth function). The dots never sit exactly on it, though. That tiny mismatch is what the whole story is about.

STEP 2 · COMPLEX NUMBERS ARE NOT COMPLEX

The imaginary direction is just sideways

To describe a centre we need two numbers: how far along the line it is, and how high up. Mathematicians write this as one number, x + i·y, and call it a complex number. The name is unlucky. Nothing is complex or imaginary about it.

Think of i as an instruction: turn a quarter of a circle. Multiply by i once and an arrow pointing right now points up. Twice, and it points left, which is why i·i = −1. The "imaginary" axis is simply the direction at right angles to the ordinary number line. It was there all along; we just usually look along the line and don't see it.

Want to see it in 3D? Complex numbers are not imaginary shows one sphere from the front and from above: from above, the imaginary part points straight at your eye and disappears.

So every circle centre is one complex number: the real part says how far along, the imaginary part is the prime itself, the height. Seen from the origin, each centre also has an angle, and the tiny change in that angle carries exactly the same wobble we are about to study. These complex numbers are not yet the zeta function's own: we will meet complex numbers again in step 5, where they set the rhythm of the primes.

STEP 3 · THE WOBBLE

The centres wobble around the curve

Measure how far each centre lies from the smooth curve. After a cluster of primes that are close together, the chain gets ahead of the curve. After a big gap between primes, it falls behind. Most of the time it sits a little behind this curve; that slight lean is a known effect, caused by squares of primes. In this model, the first time the chain catches up and crosses the dashed curve is at the circles 109 and 113, right before the big gap to 127.

Centre ahead of the curve Centre behind the curve

The wobble grows as the circles grow. Scale it by the size the theory expects, and something remarkable appears: for every prime up to 100 million, the wobble stays in the same band, swinging up and down in an irregular but strangely rhythmic way. No finite range can prove anything about infinity, of course. The computation shows what the theory predicts, not that it holds forever.

STEP 4 · THE WOBBLE IS MUSIC

Hidden notes inside the wobble

A chord on a piano is several notes at once. You can split any wobbly signal into its notes too, a bit like an ear does. Do that with the wobble of the circles, measured against the logarithm of the prime size (each step along that axis means multiplying by the same factor), and clear notes ring out at 14.13, 21.02, 25.01, 30.42, 32.94, …

Dashed lines: the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. Method: the scaled wobble for primes up to 10⁷, resampled on an even grid in ln y, Hann window, direct Fourier transform. The random numbers (Cramér's model) have the same density as the primes and go through exactly the same steps.

Those numbers are famous. They are the zeros of the Riemann zeta function, found by Bernhard Riemann in 1859. Swap the primes for random numbers of the same density and the notes vanish. The music belongs to the primes.

Each note behaves like a wheel turning at its own speed. Put wheels on wheels, one per note, and follow the tip: it reproduces the wobble. The more wheels, the closer the match.

STEP 5 · WHAT THE HYPOTHESIS SAYS

All wheels stay the same size

Riemann noticed that his zeros all seemed to lie on one straight line in the plane of complex numbers, the critical line. His hypothesis is that every zero lies there, infinitely many of them.

In the circle picture this has a simple meaning. After scaling the wobble by y1.5, a zero on the line is a wheel that keeps its size, apart from slow logarithmic factors. A zero off the line would be a wheel whose size changes like a power of y, and because zeros come in mirror pairs on either side of the line, one of the pair would always be a wheel that keeps growing. Try it: move the first zero off the line and watch what happens to the wobble far out.

A model, not data: the wobble built from the first 138 zeros, out to primes of size 10⁴⁰, to show how it grows. Real part ½ means on the critical line.

The Riemann Hypothesis, in circle language: however far you go, the wobble of the prime circles never grows faster than y1.5, the size of the band you see here. It may creep outward extremely slowly, by logarithmic factors, but never by a power of y. The primes are as regular as they can possibly be.

all zeros on Re(s) = ½ ⟺ wobble grows no faster than y1.5+ε, for every ε > 0

This follows from a classical theorem by Helge von Koch (1901) about counting primes, carried over to the circles; the short derivation is in the notes at the end.

STEP 6 · WHERE IT STANDS

Checked by computers, proved this year?

For more than 160 years nobody could prove it, although computers rigorously checked every zero up to height 3 trillion, more than 12 trillion zeros, and found every one of them on the line. In November 2026, mathematician Levent Alpöge announced a proof developed together with an AI model, written in the proof language Lean. The Lean and Mathlib communities certified it the next day. The written-out version runs past 250,000 pages, and mathematicians are now working to understand it. Some groups still dispute whether a proof nobody can read counts.

If it stands, the picture above is now a fact about all primes, forever: the circles never stray further from their curve than the wheels allow.

It would also settle far more than one question. For over a century, mathematicians have proved hundreds of results that begin with the words “assuming the Riemann Hypothesis is true”: about how primes are spread out, how large the gaps between them can be, and the speed of several number-theory algorithms. Alpöge's proof also claims the generalized version of the hypothesis. If it holds, every result that depends only on these hypotheses stops being conditional and becomes a theorem at once. Mankind would have the proof, and everything built on it, before anyone fully understands why it works.

Why I made this

Just as the mathematician behind the new proof worked with an AI model, I used Claude to write the code and run the computations for this page. My own small contribution is the circles, and the wish behind them: I want more people to understand this beautiful problem. Riemann's original framing, with the zeta function and its analytic continuation, can look forbidding. The beauty underneath it is not.

Complex numbers are one of the main building blocks of mathematics, and they are not mysterious: they are the ordinary numbers plus one quarter-turn. With that single idea, a compass and a row of prime circles, you can see what the most famous problem in mathematics is really about.

NOTES · FOR THE MATHEMATICALLY CURIOUS

What exactly is computed

This page makes no new theorem and no new proof. It is a geometric encoding of known prime-sum mathematics that makes the role of the zeta zeros visible.

Circles: radius p for every prime p ≥ 3, standing on one line, each touching the next. Touching gives a horizontal step 2√(pₖpₖ₊₁), so
xₙ = Σ 2√(pₖpₖ₊₁) = 2·Σp≤pₙ p − pₙ − 3 − Cₙ,   with Cₙ = Σ(√pₖ₊₁ − √pₖ)².
Since (√q − √p)² ≤ q − p, the tangency correction satisfies 0 ≤ Cₙ ≤ pₙ.

Smooth curve: 2·li(y²) − y − 3, from Σp≤y p ∼ li(y²).
Wobble: E(y) = x − (2·li(y²) − y − 3) = 2·(Σp≤y p − li(y²)) + O(y).

RH ⟺ π(y) = li(y) + O(y1/2+ε) (von Koch). Partial summation turns this into Σp≤y p = li(y²) + O(y3/2+ε) and back again. The O(y) tangency term is far smaller, so
RH ⟺ E(y) = O(y3/2+ε) for every ε > 0.

Waves: by the explicit formula, a zero ρ = β + iγ adds a term of size y1+β, oscillating like cos(γ·ln y + φ). Scaled by y3/2/ln y, its amplitude goes like yβ−1/2: constant on the critical line. The wheels in step 4 use the amplitude 4/|ρ + 1| predicted for β = ½.

All numbers can be reproduced with the scripts in the GitHub repository (primes up to 10⁸, zeros up to height 300).
